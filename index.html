01

About Me

I completed my MSc in Artificial Intelligence at the University of Edinburgh, and my BE in Electronics and Communication Engineering at Anna University.

I joined ZOT in February 2025 as a Software Application Engineer intern, and I have since been promoted to Software Engineer. Over a little more than a year and a half, it has been a continuous learning experience: getting to know the business from the ground up, from the work of an operator on the floor through to the priorities of the managing director, and collaborating with knowledgeable colleagues at every level. By combining their deep manufacturing expertise with what I bring on the technology side, we have been able to build and transform the way the business works from within.

I focus on understanding how people actually work, and on designing technology that fits naturally around them, so that the tools genuinely support the team and add lasting value to the business.

ZOT had exactly that profile: keen, cash-disciplined, and running production on systems that pre-dated the cloud. 03

What I found in February 2025

Each division ran its own order-management system on its own SQL Server, maintained for twenty to twenty-five years. Thousands of tables, hundreds of columns, no foreign keys, no documentation, and naming that did not agree between servers: the same field appears as no., prod_no and productNo.

Anything the systems did not cover was kept in spreadsheets. In the PCB division one workbook had been running since around 2005, on a machine that had not been switched off in years. It linked out to roughly a dozen more, each with twenty or thirty sheets, some ending in 2009, some in 2019, some starting in 2024, all tracking more or less the same thing in different formats. Thirty thousand rows, no schema, no backup, and no graph at the end of it.

Directors who wanted a view of performance exported from those systems by hand and rebuilt charts in Excel each time they needed them. The data existed. Nobody could see it.

04

Why we could not simply buy it

This was the first question the board asked, and it is a fair one. We looked at the market properly. The problem is structural, not commercial: the ERP market is organised around single lines of business, and ZOT runs three.

Sector-specific ERPs

Deep and granular for one discipline. Buying three means buying three silos, three integrations and three sets of terminology, which is what we already had.

Multi-division ERPs

Broad enough to cover all three, but shallow. They lose the process detail: plating, etch, impedance, bow and twist. That is exactly where our quality and cost live.

What we needed

Process-level detail per division and a single aggregated view across them. No vendor sells that combination, because almost nobody needs it. We do.

So the decision was not “build rather than buy” on principle. It was that the aggregated view (the thing the directors actually wanted) could only be assembled on top of the granular systems we already ran. Everything after this follows from that.

06

How the work actually gets delivered

Every project has run through the same five steps. It is not a formal framework; it is what works when you are one engineer serving eight departments and cannot afford to build the wrong thing.

  1. 01

    Find it on the floor, not in a backlog

    I spend time in each division and in accounts, HR and sales. Almost every project on the timeline started as somebody describing a task they disliked, not as an assigned requirement.

  2. 02

    Reverse-engineer the rules before writing any code

    Payroll had no rule book. The logic lived with one or two people. I ran repeated sessions with HR, accounts and payroll, wrote the rules down, and had them corrected until they were right. That document now belongs to the company, not to individuals.

  3. 03

    Prioritise openly on a shared board

    Requests from directors and engineers alike go onto one board in ClickUp, categorised and ranked. When two departments both want something first, the trade-off is visible rather than a private judgement of mine.

  4. 04

    Build on a branch, demo, correct, repeat

    Each feature is developed in isolation and shown back to the people who asked for it in short cycles: the same verification loop each time, against what they said they needed. Production is never the place where an idea gets tested.

  5. 05

    Ship it, then keep owning it

    Automated tests and code review run on every change, deployment is automated, and the desktop application on the PCB floor updates itself so nobody has to install anything. I remain the support line for all of it.

Vortex, the customer portal

Our customers are other manufacturers placing bulk orders that split across batches, divisions and process stages. Keeping them informed used to mean somebody writing emails.

Vortex gives them a secure login to their own live position: which stage each order is at, expected dates, whether it is on time, and their delivery performance over any period. For our larger accounts there is a cluster view covering every site they buy from.

It is the piece I am least aware of a direct competitor offering at our scale, and the one customers react to most.

11

Inspecting assembled boards by comparison

New board designs arrive constantly, and each one needs its optical inspection programmed before it can be checked. The assembly team's first approach was a single end-to-end vision model, run locally for security. It was expensive to set up and slow in use, because full-resolution board images either overwhelmed the model or lost the detail that mattered when scaled down.

Working with the assembly engineer who raised it, I proposed treating it as an alignment and difference problem rather than a recognition problem. The reference board and the manufactured board are aligned, components are detected and masked on both, and only the differences are scored. It is robust to how the board is placed under the camera and to changes in lighting.

Being precise about status. The pipeline is designed to reduce the current inspection setup from days to seconds, and that is the figure in my first-round entry. It is not yet in full production use, so I would describe it as designed and demonstrated rather than measured. The judges should hold me to the difference.

12

Where AI is doing real work

Each of these sits inside a process someone was already doing by hand. None of them was adopted because it was interesting; every one removed a specific task. Where the data is sensitive, the models run on our own infrastructure.

Plain English to a chart

A manager describes what they want to see; a local model turns it into a query across the divisional databases and returns the data to plot. Dynamic questions get answered without waiting for me to build a dashboard.

Recruitment screening

Identifying details are removed first. Several agents then argue the candidate's strengths and weaknesses against the job description and converge on a score with a written rationale HR can challenge.

Document extraction

Supplier, utility and landfill invoices are read automatically into the ERP at around 94% extraction accuracy, feeding the environmental and cost dashboards without manual entry.

Bill of materials matching

Customers describe the same component in different ways. A fine-tuned model normalises each description, then the system searches stock and supplier catalogues and ranks candidates by how well they match.

Forecasting

Time-series models project sales trends and electricity, water and waste usage into the next month, quarter and financial year, so environmental and cost planning starts from a projection rather than last year's number.

Payroll assistant

HR can ask why a particular payslip looks the way it does. The assistant knows the payroll rules, decides which rules and records apply, and answers from the data rather than guessing.

What went wrong along the way

Payroll, under a deadline

Salaried, hourly and weekly staff, day and night shifts, four-, five- and six-day weeks, several contracted hour patterns. The rules were undocumented and pay day does not move. The heavy read and write load became a bottleneck, and I had to optimise queries and add caching while corrections were still landing. It was the most uncomfortable delivery I have run, and the reason I now write the rules down before I write code.

Databases that do not agree

With no foreign keys and no shared naming, joining across divisions is a judgement call about which loosely-matched columns describe the same thing. I resolved it by checking every mapping with the person who enters that data, rather than by inferring it from the schema. Slower, but the numbers on the dashboards are ones the divisions recognise as their own.

A brittle first attempt at supplier data

The obvious way to search supplier catalogues is to scrape their websites. Scrapers break the moment a page changes, and I did not want a core purchasing tool that failed silently. I went back and arranged proper access with the suppliers instead, which took longer and is still working.

Asking the right questions

The hardest part of the first few months was not technical. It was working out what colleagues actually needed from a system, and how it should be presented. I worried my questions were too complicated or beside the point. People were patient with me, and learning to ask better questions has improved every project since.

15

Beyond my own remit

The part I am most pleased about is not a system. It is that colleagues now bring me ideas. Marketing worked with me on the website. HR asked for the recruitment pipeline and now uses it for everything. Sales use the costed bill of materials for pricing. The job cards started as a conversation with a colleague on the PCB floor, and we took the proposal to the Managing Director together.

Some of it is quieter capability building. The payroll rules and the database map existed only in one or two people's heads. Writing them down and putting them in a system means the company owns that knowledge now, and it does not leave when someone does.

Outside the business, ZOT encouraged me to attend research conferences and technical meetups, where I could learn from engineers with far more experience than me. My placement was published as a case study by the Midlothian and East Lothian Chamber of Commerce, and I am glad it is being used to make the case for taking on technical interns in manufacturing. That route is how I got here.

What comes next

The foundation is in place, which means the next twelve months are about depth rather than plumbing.

Job cards, end to end

Complete the PCB rollout so every board carries a full digital paper trail, then feed that process timing back into lead-time and capacity planning.

Inspection into production

Take the inspection pipeline from demonstrated to running on the line, with the assembly team, and start measuring it properly instead of projecting.

Machine data

The flow meters proved the pattern cheaply. Extending live machine data into the ERP is the logical next step, and the one that turns reporting into monitoring.

A deeper Vortex tier

A richer portal for our largest accounts, with more of the quality and delivery evidence they currently have to ask us for.

Not being the only engineer

One person is a risk to a platform this load-bearing. Documenting it properly, and eventually having someone alongside me, matters more than any single feature on this list.
